Chapter Four

Chunking: the lever nobody talks about.

Chunking decides what the retriever can see. Everything downstream — embeddings, vector search, generation — can only work with the pieces the chunker handed over. A good chunker beats a better embedder more often than the reverse.

§ 4.0  ·  why chunking exists at all

The problem in one sentence

Language models cannot read your whole corpus. Even models with huge context windows are too slow, too expensive, and too easily distracted to answer well when you dump millions of tokens on them. So the retriever’s job is to find the small handful of pieces that actually matter, and hand only those to the model.

The chunker is the thing that decides what a “piece” is. If you cut a paragraph in half, the retriever will only ever match on one half at a time. If you make chunks too small, they lose the context needed to make sense. Too big, and irrelevant content dilutes the signal.

This is why chunking dominates. You can have the best embedder in the world, but if it is embedding broken half-sentences, none of that quality reaches the user.

§ 4.1  ·  the naive baseline

Fixed-size chunking

The simplest possible strategy: count tokens, cut every N. If N is 512, every chunk is 512 tokens long, no matter what.

This is fast and reproducible. It is also indifferent to the content — it will happily cut through the middle of a sentence, chop a code block in half, or split a paragraph so the topic sentence lives in one chunk and the supporting detail in another. The retriever, later, will match on one half or the other, never both.

Fixed-size is what you use when you need a baseline to measure everything else against, not what you ship.

§ 4.2  ·  the industry default

Recursive character splitting

Recursive splitting is a small idea with a big effect: instead of cutting at a fixed length, prefer cutting at natural boundaries when possible. The classic implementation is a ranked list of separators to try, in order:

  1. First, try to split on double-newlines (paragraph breaks).
  2. If a chunk is still too big, split on single newlines (line breaks).
  3. If still too big, split on sentence terminators (., ?, !).
  4. Then on spaces.
  5. Finally, characters — the last resort.

The recursion is greedy: at each level, split into pieces, and any piece still over the size limit gets split by the next-finer separator. Most chunks end up ending at paragraph or sentence boundaries. A few too-long paragraphs get further subdivided.

This is what LangChain’s RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter does. It is boring, cheap, and much better than fixed-size for almost no extra work. If you have not thought about chunking, this is your starting point.

§ 4.3  ·  using meaning to decide

Semantic chunking

Recursive splitting respects punctuation but not meaning. Two paragraphs might both look like normal prose to a text splitter, even though they are about completely different topics. Ideally you want to cut where the topic changes.

Semantic chunking does exactly that. The recipe:

  1. Split the document into sentences.
  2. Embed each sentence.
  3. For each pair of adjacent sentences, compute the distance between their embeddings.
  4. Where the distance suddenly jumps — that is a topic boundary. Cut there.

The intuition: sentences about the same topic have similar embeddings, so their pairwise distances stay small. Cross a topic boundary, and the next sentence lands in a different neighbourhood — the distance spikes. That spike is your signal.

More concretely, you compute the distances, look at the distribution, and cut at, say, the top 5% of distances. This gives you variable-length chunks aligned with real topic boundaries.

The cost

Semantic chunking costs an embedding call per sentence at ingestion time. That is real money for large corpora. It also depends on the quality of the embedder you use for chunking — garbage embeddings, garbage boundaries. In practice, semantic chunking is worth it for narrative content (articles, reports, transcripts) and less useful for highly structured content where boundaries are already obvious.

§ 4.4  ·  a small trick with big payoff

Sliding window overlap

Every chunking strategy has an edge problem: what if the answer lives right on a boundary? Half the sentence is in chunk 47, half is in chunk 48. Neither chunk retrieves cleanly.

The fix is to let chunks overlap. Instead of chunk 47 ending exactly where chunk 48 begins, they share some tail-and-head content — typically 10 to 20 percent of the chunk size.

The math is simple. If your chunk size is 512 tokens and you use 15% overlap, each chunk overlaps its neighbour by roughly 77 tokens. For a document of \(T\) tokens, the number of chunks becomes:

\[ N_{\text{chunks}} \approx \frac{T}{\text{size} - \text{overlap}} \]

Overlap increases storage (you are storing some tokens more than once), but the effect on retrieval quality is usually worth it, especially for narrative content. You do need to deduplicate at retrieval time — if a query matches two overlapping chunks with the same core content, you do not want to return both.

Rule of thumb: 15% overlap for prose, less for structured content where boundaries already carry meaning.

§ 4.5  ·  the biggest quality unlock

Parent-child chunking (small-to-big)

There is a fundamental tension in chunk sizing. Small chunks give precise retrieval — the embedding is focused, matches are sharp. Large chunks give rich generation context — the LLM has the surrounding paragraphs, not just the matching sentence.

Parent-child chunking sidesteps the tension by doing both. You keep two representations of the same content:

  • Children: small chunks (say, one sentence each). These go into the vector index. Retrieval scores against these.
  • Parents: larger chunks (a full paragraph or section). Stored separately, linked by ID.

At query time: retrieve the top-k child chunks by similarity. Look up each child’s parent. Return the parents to the LLM.

You get precise matching (children are small and focused) and rich context (parents are large enough to make sense on their own). For long-form question answering, this often produces a bigger quality jump than any change to your embedder.

Bookkeeping matters

Store every child chunk with a parent_id pointing to its containing chunk. At retrieval, deduplicate on parent_id — if three child chunks all point to the same parent, return that parent once, not three times. This is one of those cases where good schema design at ingestion time saves ugly logic later.

§ 4.6  ·  retrieval at multiple scales

Hierarchical chunking (RAPTOR)

Parent-child gives you two levels. RAPTOR — Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval — gives you many. The idea is to build a tree of abstractions, and retrieve at whichever level answers the question.

The recipe:

  1. Chunk the corpus normally at the base level.
  2. Embed the chunks and cluster them by topic (e.g. with Gaussian Mixture Models or K-means).
  3. For each cluster, ask an LLM to write a summary of the chunks in that cluster.
  4. Add those summaries to the index as new nodes.
  5. Now cluster the summaries. Summarise those. Add to the index.
  6. Repeat until you get to a single root summary of the whole corpus.

Now the index contains chunks (detail), cluster summaries (mid-level), and higher-order summaries (big picture) — all in the same vector space. A specific question retrieves the detailed chunks; a broad question like “what does this corpus say about X overall” retrieves the higher-level summaries.

RAPTOR is powerful for cross-document synthesis but expensive to build: you are running LLM calls at every level of the tree. Rebuild costs get large as the corpus grows, so it works best on slower-changing collections.

§ 4.7  ·  letting the content decide

Adaptive and agentic chunking

One chunking strategy for everything is a compromise. Real corpora are heterogeneous: prose, code, tables, structured records, forms. Each has different natural boundaries.

Adaptive chunking means routing content to different chunkers based on type. Code is chunked at function or class boundaries. Tables are kept whole and represented as Markdown. Prose goes through recursive or semantic. This is not fancy — it is just refusing to use one hammer on every nail.

Agentic chunking takes it further. Instead of using rules to find boundaries, you ask an LLM to read the document and identify “self-contained propositions” — passages that make sense on their own without external context. Each proposition becomes a chunk.

The quality is high because the LLM understands what “makes sense on its own” means. The cost is also high — an LLM call per document at ingestion time is not cheap. Reserve this for high-value, low-volume corpora: legal contracts, medical guidelines, curated knowledge bases.

§ 4.8  ·  the settings that matter

The knobs, and what they do

Every chunker has three knobs worth understanding.

Chunk size

Usually somewhere between 256 and 1024 tokens. Smaller chunks give more precise retrieval and produce more chunks (higher storage, more index entries). Larger chunks give the LLM more context per hit but dilute the embedding signal — a 1500-token chunk covering three topics will not match well on any of them.

Overlap

Typically 10-20% of chunk size. Larger for narrative content where ideas flow across boundaries; smaller for structured content where cutting at a boundary loses nothing.

Boundary respect

Never split inside a sentence. Never split inside a code block. Never split inside a table. These constraints override chunk size — better a slightly oversized chunk than a mangled one.

Chapter Five

Vector databases: storing the billions.

Once you have vectors, you need to find the nearest neighbours of a query vector across possibly billions of them, in tens of milliseconds. This is the whole problem vector databases exist to solve, and the way they solve it — approximate nearest neighbour indexes — is worth understanding in real detail.

§ 5.0  ·  the impossibility of brute force

Why we cannot just check every vector

Suppose you have a billion 768-dimensional vectors. To find the nearest neighbour of a query, you have to compute the similarity between the query and each vector. That is:

\[ \text{cost} = O(N \cdot d) = O(10^9 \cdot 768) \approx 10^{12} \text{ multiplications} \]

Even on modern hardware, that is many seconds per query. Not tolerable. So we accept a small loss of accuracy in exchange for enormous speedups. This is the trade at the heart of the field — approximate nearest neighbour search, or ANN.

The rest of this chapter is about how the trade is structured. We look at each major index type, what problem it solves, and what it costs.

§ 5.1  ·  the baseline you always keep

Flat (brute force) indexes

Flat means no index at all — just store the vectors and compare the query to every one. Slow, but exact. You always get the true top-k.

Two reasons to care. First, up to about a million vectors on modern hardware, flat is fast enough for many use cases. Second, and more important: flat is the ground truth for measuring the accuracy of any approximate method. You cannot evaluate your HNSW recall without something to compare its results against.

Always keep a flat baseline available for a subset of your data. It is the only way to know if your approximate index is any good.

§ 5.2  ·  the clustering approach

IVF: divide the space, search only the neighbourhood

The first big idea. Instead of comparing the query to every vector, first cluster the vectors into groups (called cells or inverted lists). Then, at query time, only search inside the cells nearest to the query.

The recipe:

  1. Run k-means on the vectors, producing nlist clusters. Each vector belongs to one cluster (its nearest centroid).
  2. To answer a query, compute the query’s distance to each centroid.
  3. Take the nprobe closest centroids.
  4. Only compare the query against vectors in those cells.

If nlist is 1024 and nprobe is 8, you search roughly \(8/1024 \approx 0.8\%\) of your data. That is a huge speedup. Query cost drops from \(O(N \cdot d)\) to approximately:

\[ O\left(\text{nprobe} \cdot \frac{N}{\text{nlist}} \cdot d + \text{nlist} \cdot d\right) \]

The two terms are: distance to centroids (searching \(\text{nlist}\) of them), plus vectors inside the chosen cells.

The trade is transparent: raise nprobe for higher recall (more cells searched, more candidates considered) and lower QPS. Lower nprobe for the opposite. This is why IVF is loved — the trade is easy to tune.

The failure mode

IVF misses the true nearest neighbour when the query is close to a cell boundary. Its actual nearest neighbour might live one cell over. Raising nprobe helps but never guarantees. This is why real deployments rarely use IVF alone — it usually gets combined with something else.

§ 5.3  ·  compression, cleverly

Product Quantization

Vectors are large. A single 768-dimensional float32 vector takes \(768 \times 4 = 3072\) bytes. A billion of them takes about 3 terabytes. You cannot fit that in RAM. Even on SSD it is huge and slow.

Product Quantization compresses vectors dramatically by taking advantage of a subtle insight: you do not need to store the exact vector to find nearest neighbours — you only need to compute distances approximately.

The recipe:

  1. Split each \(d\)-dimensional vector into \(m\) sub-vectors of dimension \(d/m\). For \(d = 768,\; m = 8\), each sub-vector is 96-dimensional.
  2. For each of the \(m\) sub-vector positions, run k-means over all training vectors to build a codebook of, say, \(k = 256\) centroids.
  3. Now, to encode a vector, replace each sub-vector with the index (0–255) of its nearest centroid in the corresponding codebook.
  4. Each vector becomes \(m\) bytes.

Concretely: your 3072-byte vector becomes 8 bytes — a 384× compression. A billion vectors fit in 8 GB.

To compute distances, you precompute the distance between the query and every centroid in every codebook (a small table). Then approximate the distance between the query and any encoded vector by looking up \(m\) precomputed distances and summing them:

\[ \text{dist}(q, x) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{m} \text{dist}(q_i, c_{i, \text{code}_i(x)}) \]
Where c is the centroid at the i-th codebook indexed by the vector's i-th code

No floating-point multiplications on the vector itself — just \(m\) lookups and a sum. This is why PQ is fast even without a GPU.

OPQ, the small improvement that matters

PQ works best when the sub-vectors are uncorrelated — when each sub-vector captures a distinct aspect of meaning. In practice, the raw dimensions of an embedding are usually not aligned that way. OPQ (Optimized Product Quantization) learns a rotation matrix during training that rotates the vectors so their sub-vectors are as decorrelated as possible before quantizing. Same compression, better recall.

§ 5.4  ·  the on-disk workhorse

IVF-PQ: clustering plus compression

IVF makes search fast by narrowing where you look. PQ makes it cheap by shrinking what you store. Combined, IVF-PQ handles enormous corpora on modest hardware. It is the dominant on-disk approach for billion-scale retrieval.

Rough shape: use IVF to route the query to the right cells, then use PQ-compressed vectors within those cells to compute approximate distances. You get about \(10^9\) vectors on a single machine at reasonable recall.

This is what FAISS ships as IndexIVFPQ. It is what powers most of the large-scale open systems in production.

§ 5.5  ·  the graph approach

HNSW: navigating a small world

HNSW — Hierarchical Navigable Small World — is the most popular in-memory ANN algorithm. Its recall is excellent and its query latency is low. It is what most modern vector databases use by default.

The idea in one sentence: build a graph where similar vectors are connected, then search by walking.

Concretely, here is the picture.

The graph has multiple layers. The top layer is sparse — a few nodes with long connections between distant regions of the space. Each lower layer has more nodes and denser, shorter connections. The bottom layer contains every vector.

To search:

  1. Start at a designated entry point in the top layer.
  2. Greedily walk to whichever connected neighbour is closest to the query.
  3. Keep walking until no neighbour is closer than the current node — you have found the local minimum for this layer.
  4. Drop down to the next layer and repeat, using the current node as the new entry point.
  5. At the bottom layer, the local minimum is (approximately) your nearest neighbour.

Why the layers help: the top layer lets you take huge leaps across the space quickly. As you descend, you refine within smaller regions. You never have to look at more than a tiny fraction of the graph. This is what makes HNSW roughly \(O(\log N)\) in the number of vectors.

The parameters

  • M: the number of neighbours each node has at each layer. Typically 16 to 64. Higher M gives better recall but uses more memory.
  • ef_construction: the size of the candidate list used when building the graph. Typically 200–500. Higher means a better graph, longer build time.
  • ef_search: the size of the candidate list used at query time. This is the runtime knob for the recall/latency trade. Higher ef_search means more candidates considered per query, better recall, slower.

Memory footprint

Roughly, each vector costs:

\[ \text{bytes per vector} \approx d \cdot 4 + M \cdot 8 \]

The first term is the raw vector (float32). The second is the graph edges — \(M\) pointers per layer, and vectors on average have a few layers. For \(d = 768, M = 32\), that is roughly 3.3 KB per vector, or 3.3 GB per million.

Where HNSW breaks

HNSW handles inserts cleanly but not deletes. Removing a node leaves “holes” in the graph — tombstoned entries that still occupy the structure. Over time, deletes accumulate and recall degrades. The remedy is periodic rebuild. If your corpus churns heavily, plan for it.

§ 5.6  ·  the specialists

DiskANN and ScaNN

HNSW is superb when your graph fits in RAM. Once your corpus grows past that, you have two options: shard across machines, or move to a disk-friendly algorithm.

DiskANN (from Microsoft Research, sometimes called Vamana) is a graph index designed to live on SSD. The graph structure is tuned so that a query touches only a small number of pages on disk, and the memory usage is a small fraction of the vectors. A single machine with an SSD can hold a billion vectors and answer queries in tens of milliseconds. This is what many billion-scale systems use.

ScaNN (from Google) attacks the problem from a different angle. Its central innovation is anisotropic quantization: when compressing vectors, minimize the error in the direction that matters most for maximum inner product search — not the overall error uniformly. If a component of the vector matters more for computing inner products, spend more of your quantization budget on preserving it. This gives excellent recall at low storage.

In interviews, knowing which company built which is a useful thread. DiskANN is Microsoft. ScaNN is Google. FAISS is Meta. Each ships as a research-grade artefact and gets wrapped by vendors.

§ 5.7  ·  combining vectors with structured fields

Metadata filtering

Real queries almost always come with structured constraints. “Find me support tickets similar to this one, but only in the last 30 days, and only from Enterprise customers.” The vector search handles “similar to this one.” The filter handles the rest.

Combining them is harder than it looks. There are three approaches.

Pre-filter

First apply the filter to shrink the candidate set. Then run vector search only on what remains. Correct results but potentially slow — if the filter is very selective (say, 0.1% of the corpus matches), you might end up doing something close to brute force on that subset because the ANN index cannot help.

Post-filter

Run vector search first to get the top-K candidates, then apply the filter to what came back. Fast, but broken if none of the top-K happen to match your filter — you get empty results even though relevant documents exist elsewhere.

Filtered search (native)

The best modern approach: integrate the filter into the ANN traversal itself. As HNSW walks the graph, it only considers nodes that pass the filter. Nothing is retrieved that would be filtered out anyway, and the search visits exactly the right region of the graph.

Qdrant, Weaviate, and Pinecone all support native filtered search. If your workload is filter-heavy, this is the feature that matters most in your database choice.

§ 5.9  ·  measuring goodness

The metric everyone should track

Recall@k measures what fraction of the true top-k neighbours (as computed by brute force) your approximate index actually returned.

\[ \text{Recall@k} = \frac{|\, \text{ANN}_k \cap \text{true}_k \,|}{k} \]

Where \(\text{ANN}_k\) is the top-k from your approximate index, and \(\text{true}_k\) is the true top-k from an exact (flat) search.

Every ANN system has a recall/latency curve. Higher recall means visiting more candidates, which means slower queries. Where you land on that curve is a choice, not a fixed property of the algorithm. Reporting your target Recall@k alongside your latency is the honest way to describe performance.

Chapter Six

Query processing: bridging user and corpus.

Users do not write like documents. Documents do not answer questions the way questions are asked. Between the two is a translation problem — and where that translation goes wrong is where most retrieval systems actually fail.

§ 6.0  ·  the gap

The intent-vs-surface problem

A user types: “what about Q4?” They mean, based on the prior conversation, “what was Apple’s revenue in Q4 2024?” But your retriever sees the literal string “what about Q4?” No document on earth says that.

Or the user types “how do I stop my dog from barking at the mailman.” The document that answers this says “canine reactivity to visitors can be modified through counter-conditioning protocols.” No shared vocabulary. Dense embedding helps, but only some.

Query processing is the layer that reshapes the user’s question into something a retriever can actually work with. Its techniques are various, and picking the right one for your workload is more important than most people give it credit for.

§ 6.1  ·  making the query self-contained

Query rewriting

Query rewriting is the simplest bridge: pass the user’s raw query (along with the conversation history) to an LLM and ask it to produce a self-contained, search-friendly version.

This is especially important in multi-turn conversations. The first question, “how did Apple perform last year?”, is fine. The follow-up, “what about Q4?”, is not searchable on its own. A rewriter turns it into “Apple financial performance Q4 2024” using context from the earlier turn.

The prompt is usually simple: give the LLM the conversation history and the raw query, ask it to produce a standalone query optimised for search. Return that as the string you actually embed and match.

The cost you pay

Rewriting adds an LLM call to every query — typically 200 to 500 milliseconds of latency. For interactive systems that matters. Two mitigations: cache aggressively (many queries look alike), and use a small distilled model just for rewriting rather than your main generator. Always log the rewritten query alongside the original; when a user reports bad results, the rewrite is the first thing you want to see.

§ 6.2  ·  casting a wider net

Query expansion

Expansion means adding related terms to the query to catch documents that use different vocabulary. There are two traditions.

Rocchio pseudo-relevance feedback

A classic IR technique. The intuition: run the search once, take the top few results, assume they are probably relevant, and pull the query embedding in their direction. Formally:

\[ q_{\text{new}} = \alpha \cdot q + \beta \cdot \frac{1}{|D_r|} \sum_{d \in D_r} d - \gamma \cdot \frac{1}{|D_n|} \sum_{d \in D_n} d \]

Where \(D_r\) is the set of assumed-relevant top results, \(D_n\) is the assumed-non-relevant tail, and \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) are weights (typically \(\alpha \approx 1, \beta \approx 0.75, \gamma \approx 0.15\)). The updated query is closer to the relevant documents and further from the irrelevant ones. You re-run search with the new query.

LLM-based expansion

Simpler and often better: ask an LLM for related terms and synonyms, and append them to the query. For a query about “heart attack,” the LLM adds “myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, MI, chest pain.” The expanded query catches documents using medical terminology the user did not know.

§ 6.3  ·  the counterintuitive trick

HyDE: retrieve using a fake answer

HyDE stands for Hypothetical Document Embeddings, and it is the most interesting technique in this chapter. The setup:

  1. Take the user’s query.
  2. Ask an LLM to write what a plausible answer would look like. Do not check whether the answer is actually correct.
  3. Embed the fake answer.
  4. Use the fake answer’s embedding as the query for retrieval.

The trick is that a fake answer looks stylistically much more like a real document than a question does. Queries and documents live in different regions of embedding space — questions are short, have interrogative structure, and phrase things from the outside. Answers are declarative, longer, and use the vocabulary the target documents use.

By embedding a fake answer, you jump into the “document neighbourhood” of the embedding space, where the real documents live. Retrieval finds them more easily.

HyDE works remarkably well zero-shot — no fine-tuning, no training data. It is also one of the cheapest quality improvements you can make.

When HyDE hurts

HyDE fails on queries that are already document-shaped. FAQs (“What is the return policy?”) and definitional queries (“Define machine learning”) look enough like docs that generating a fake answer adds noise rather than useful vocabulary. If your traffic is mostly FAQ-shaped, HyDE is not worth the LLM call.

§ 6.4  ·  safety in numbers

Multi-query retrieval

Instead of relying on one phrasing of the query, generate several. Ask an LLM to produce three to five paraphrases, run retrieval for each, and union the results (deduplicating along the way).

For a query “how do I train a puppy not to bite,” you might get:

  • “puppy bite training methods”
  • “stopping mouthing behaviour in young dogs”
  • “bite inhibition training for puppies”
  • “how to teach a puppy not to nip”

Each paraphrase retrieves a slightly different set. Their union covers more ground than any single query could. This is a simple, robust technique — it usually helps and rarely hurts.

The cost, again, is latency and LLM calls. Cache paraphrases for common queries; run them in parallel where the retriever supports it.

§ 6.5  ·  teaching the query to filter itself

Self-query retrieval

Users often mix semantic and structured constraints in one sentence: “papers about diffusion models published after 2022” or “support tickets from Enterprise customers related to billing.”

Self-query retrieval uses an LLM to parse such queries into two pieces:

  • A semantic query: the part you actually embed and match.
  • A structured filter: the constraints applied to metadata.

“Papers about diffusion models published after 2022” becomes: 

{
  "semantic_query": "diffusion models",
  "filter": { "year": { "$gte": 2022 } }
}

Then you run a filtered vector search using both pieces. This handles the mixed-intent case cleanly — the semantic part matches on content, the filter part enforces the constraint.

The LLM prompt needs to know your schema (which fields exist and what values they take). Give it the schema in the system prompt and it can usually extract filters reliably.

§ 6.6  ·  the boring but critical layer

Spell correction and domain synonyms

A user searching an e-commerce catalogue for “iphonr charger” will get zero results if you match literally. That is unacceptable. Spell correction is table stakes for any search product.

Approaches include BK-trees, SymSpell (very fast, precomputes deletes within a Levenshtein distance), and, increasingly, LLM-based correction. For any real product, spell correction should sit in front of the retrieval layer and quietly fix common typos.

In specialist domains, you often need synonym expansion beyond what a general model knows. Medical retrieval maps ICD codes and SNOMED terms. Legal retrieval maps terms of art. E-commerce maps brand names and product categories. These are usually curated dictionaries maintained separately from your retrieval model.

§ 6.7  ·  different queries want different pipelines

Intent detection and routing

Not every query wants the same retrieval strategy. A FAQ-style query wants a short direct answer from a curated FAQ index. A long-form research question wants many chunks stitched together from a large corpus. A code question wants a code-specific index with function-level chunking.

Intent detection is a small classifier — often just an LLM call — that labels the query before retrieval. The label routes the query to a specific backend. Each backend can then be tuned for its class of question, with its own chunker, embedder, and retriever configuration.

This is one of those architectural choices that unlocks a lot of downstream flexibility. It also demands more infrastructure than a single-pipeline system, so it is only worth it once you actually have distinguishable query classes with distinguishable needs.

Query processing is where the user meets the retriever. Every technique in this chapter is a small translation. Together, they close a gap that would otherwise show up as “the answer looked right but was for the wrong question.”
