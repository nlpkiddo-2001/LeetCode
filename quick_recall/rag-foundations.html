A study companion · three chapters

The Retrieval Notebook: from term counts to semantic search.

Every argument about RAG — vector-only versus hybrid, dense versus sparse, why re-ranking matters — eventually collapses back to a small set of classical ideas. This notebook walks through those ideas slowly and plainly, math included, so that by the end the arguments make sense on their own terms.

\[ \text{score}(d, q) = \sum_{t \in q} \underbrace{\text{IDF}(t)}_{\color{#2E5266}\text{rarity}} \cdot \frac{\overbrace{f(t,d)}^{\color{#B8722E}\text{count}} (k_1 + 1)}{f(t,d) + \underbrace{k_1 \left(1 - b + b \cdot \tfrac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}} \right)}_{\color{#6B8E4E}\text{length penalty}}} \]
IDF(t) How rare the word is across all documents. Rare words carry more weight.
f(t,d) How many times the word appears in this specific document.
k₁ A knob controlling how quickly extra occurrences stop mattering.
|d| / avgdl How long this document is, compared to the average. Longer documents get pulled down.

That formula is BM25. It is the workhorse of text search, and if you understand every symbol above by the time we finish Chapter One, everything else in this document will feel easier.

Chapter One

Foundations of information retrieval.

Before there was RAG, there was search. Every retrieval system, from Google circa 1998 to a modern vector database, is trying to solve the same three-step problem. We start there.

§ 1.1  ·  the setup

What is retrieval, actually?

Imagine you have a room with a hundred million books, and someone walks in and says: “I want to read about photosynthesis in deep-sea creatures.” Your job is to hand them the ten most useful books — fast. That is the whole problem of information retrieval.

Every retrieval system, no matter how sophisticated, does this in three stages:

  1. Indexing. Before anyone asks anything, you organize the books — extract text, break it into pieces, build data structures that let you look things up quickly. This happens offline.
  2. Matching. When a query arrives, you narrow the hundred million down to some manageable set of candidates — say, the top thousand books that might be relevant.
  3. Ranking. You reorder those candidates so the best ones come first.

A few words are worth defining precisely because they will show up everywhere. A document is a single unit that can be returned — a book, an article, a paragraph, a chunk. A query is what the user asked. Relevance is a judgment about how well a document answers the query. Precision asks: of the documents we returned, how many were actually relevant? Recall asks: of all the relevant documents in existence, how many did we return? These two are usually in tension. Return everything and recall is perfect but precision is zero. Return one thing and precision is likely high but recall is terrible.

A quiet assumption

Classical IR is built on the Cranfield paradigm: a document is judged relevant to a query independently of what other documents are around it. That assumption is why we can rank documents by score, one at a time. It is also why modern approaches like “diversify the results” or “deduplicate near-duplicates” feel like patches bolted on top — because they are.

§ 1.2  ·  the first useful score

Counting words, but cleverly: TF-IDF

The first honest attempt at scoring documents was: count how often the query words appear in each document. If someone searches for photosynthesis, prefer the document that mentions photosynthesis more times. This count is called term frequency, or TF.

TF alone is not enough, and the reason is small but important. Suppose the query is “the cat sat on the mat.” The word the appears in nearly every document ever written. Ranking by raw TF would push documents heavy in the to the top, which is nonsense. We need to punish words that appear everywhere.

This is where document frequency, or DF, comes in: it counts how many documents contain a given word. the has a huge DF. photosynthesis has a tiny one. If we take the reciprocal, big DF becomes a small number, and small DF becomes a big one — that is inverse document frequency, IDF.

Because raw ratios grow too fast, we wrap it in a logarithm to keep the values sane:

\[ \text{IDF}(t) = \log\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right) \]
N is the total number of documents; df(t) is how many contain term t

Multiply TF and IDF together and you get the classic score:

\[ \text{tf-idf}(t, d) = \text{tf}(t, d) \cdot \log\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)} \]
Intuition

TF-IDF rewards words that are frequent inside a document but rare across the collection. A word that shows up ten times in this document but almost nowhere else is a strong signal that this document is about that word.

§ 1.3  ·  the workhorse

BM25: TF-IDF grown up

TF-IDF works but has two obvious flaws that anyone who has stared at real data will notice.

Flaw one: term frequency grows without limit. If photosynthesis appears once in a document, that is meaningful. If it appears a hundred times, the document is definitely about photosynthesis. But if it appears two hundred times, is that document twice as relevant as the hundred-times one? No. There are diminishing returns — the tenth mention tells you less than the second did.

Flaw two: long documents cheat. A very long document will naturally contain more occurrences of any word, just by virtue of being long. Without correction, long documents dominate the rankings even when they are not more focused on the topic.

BM25 — short for “Best Matching 25,” the 25th attempt in a research programme at Cambridge in the 1990s — fixes both. It looks intimidating on first read, so let us build it in pieces.

Piece one: saturation

Instead of using term frequency raw, wrap it in a function that flattens out:

\[ \frac{f(t,d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t,d) + k_1} \]

When \(f(t,d)\) is small, this behaves like the raw count. When \(f(t,d)\) is huge, it approaches a ceiling of \(k_1 + 1\). The parameter \(k_1\), usually set between 1.2 and 2.0, controls how quickly you approach that ceiling. Small \(k_1\) means the count saturates fast; large \(k_1\) means it keeps growing longer.

Piece two: length normalisation

To handle long documents, BM25 replaces the constant \(k_1\) in the denominator with a length-aware version:

\[ k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right) \]

Here \(|d|\) is the length of this document (in words), and avgdl is the average document length in the collection. If the document is exactly average length, the term in parentheses equals 1 and nothing changes. If the document is twice as long as average, the denominator grows, which shrinks the score. The parameter \(b\), usually 0.75, controls how aggressively long documents get penalised. Set \(b = 0\) and length is ignored entirely.

Piece three: put it together

Sum this across every query term, and weight each term by a slightly smoothed IDF:

\[ \text{score}(d,q) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t,d)(k_1 + 1)}{f(t,d) + k_1\left(1 - b + b\frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)} \]

The IDF used in BM25 is slightly different from vanilla — it is smoothed to stay positive:

\[ \text{IDF}(t) = \log\left(\frac{N - \text{df}(t) + 0.5}{\text{df}(t) + 0.5} + 1\right) \]
The senior-engineer note

BM25 is not just “TF-IDF with more knobs.” It is derived from a probabilistic model of text called the 2-Poisson model, which assumes there are two kinds of documents (those about the query topic and those not) and models the word counts in each. The formula falls out of that model with reasonable approximations. If someone in an interview tells you BM25 is just heuristic, you will know they have not read Robertson.

§ 1.4  ·  the other approach

Dense retrieval: retrieval by meaning

BM25 is beautiful but it has a ceiling. It matches words. If the user asks about heart attack and the document says myocardial infarction, BM25 will not connect them. There is no shared vocabulary.

Dense retrieval sidesteps this by turning text into vectors — long lists of numbers — where similar meanings live near each other. You pass the query through a neural network and get, say, a 768-dimensional vector. You do the same for every document. To find matches, you ask: which document vector is closest to my query vector?

Now “heart attack” and “myocardial infarction” end up in nearly the same place in this vector space, and the retrieval works even without any shared words.

The standard architecture is a dual encoder (also called a bi-encoder): one network encodes queries, another encodes documents, and you score with inner products. For symmetric tasks like “find sentences with similar meaning,” both encoders are usually the same tower. For asymmetric tasks like question-answering (short query, long passage), you sometimes train two distinct towers because queries and documents look different.

The tradeoff

Dense retrieval is great at synonyms, paraphrases, and semantic drift. It is bad at exact matches: product SKUs, part numbers, code identifiers, unusual names. If a user searches for SKU-8842-B, a dense retriever will happily return documents about vaguely similar SKUs. This is why serious systems combine dense and sparse.

§ 1.5  ·  learned sparse

Sparse retrieval isn't just BM25 anymore

When people say “sparse retrieval” they historically mean BM25 — the representation of a document is a giant vector indexed by every word in the vocabulary, mostly filled with zeros, with non-zero entries only for the words that actually appear.

Modern learned sparse retrievers like SPLADE and uniCOIL keep the sparse structure but let a neural network decide which terms to activate. Crucially, the model can activate terms that were not in the original document — a form of query and document expansion. A document about “canines” can get its dogs slot activated, so a query for dogs will find it.

You get the interpretability of BM25 (you can look at which terms are active and understand why) combined with something like the semantic reach of dense retrieval.

§ 1.6  ·  hybrid retrieval

Combining dense and sparse

Dense retrieval and BM25 fail in different ways, so combining them almost always beats either alone. The question is how to combine.

Method one: Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)

RRF ignores the raw scores entirely and looks only at the rank that each retriever gave a document. If a document is 3rd according to BM25 and 5th according to the dense retriever, its RRF score is:

\[ \text{score}(d) = \sum_i \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_i(d)} \]

Where \(k\) is a small constant, conventionally 60. The whole point is that BM25 scores and cosine similarities live on completely different scales — BM25 might return 12.4 and 8.7, while cosine returns 0.83 and 0.79. Adding those numbers directly is meaningless. Adding their reciprocal ranks is scale-free and robust.

Method two: convex combination

Alternatively, normalise both scores to the same range and take a weighted average:

\[ \text{score}(d) = \alpha \cdot \text{score}_{\text{dense}}(d) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{score}_{\text{sparse}}(d) \]

Here \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) is a tunable weight. This is more expressive than RRF — you can give dense retrieval 70% weight if it is doing more work on your domain — but it is sensitive to how you normalise the scores. Get the normalisation wrong and one signal drowns the other.

When to reach for which

RRF is the safe default. It just works. Convex combination is worth the extra effort when you have an evaluation set and know exactly how much weight each retriever deserves. Most production systems start with RRF and only migrate when they hit its ceiling.

§ 1.7  ·  measuring closeness

Similarity metrics, and when they agree

Once you have vectors, you need a way to measure how close two of them are. There are three you will see, and they are related in ways that matter.

Cosine similarity

Measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring their length.

\[ \cos(A, B) = \frac{A \cdot B}{\|A\| \cdot \|B\|} \]

A value of 1 means the vectors point in exactly the same direction; 0 means they are perpendicular (unrelated); -1 means opposite.

Dot product

Just multiply corresponding entries and sum.

\[ A \cdot B = \sum_i A_i B_i \]

Unlike cosine, dot product cares about magnitude. A vector with large numbers will produce a large dot product regardless of direction. Older embedding models sometimes deliberately made confident answers longer, so dot product was more informative than cosine. Modern models are usually normalised.

Euclidean distance (L2)

Straight-line distance in space.

\[ \|A - B\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i (A_i - B_i)^2} \]

The identity that connects them

If both vectors are unit-normalised (length 1), something clean happens. Cosine equals dot product, because dividing by \(\|A\| \cdot \|B\| = 1\) does nothing. And L2 distance is exactly determined by cosine:

\[ \|A - B\|_2^2 = 2 - 2\cos(A, B) \]

So on unit vectors, ranking by cosine, by dot product, or by inverse L2 gives you the same ordering. This matters because different vector databases default to different metrics — if you normalise up front, it does not matter which one you choose.

Trap

Do not mix. Do not train with cosine, index with dot product on un-normalised vectors, and expect the same rankings. If magnitude carries meaning in your model, dot product is right and cosine throws away signal. If magnitude is noise, normalise everything and pick whichever metric is cheapest for your index.

§ 1.8  ·  the search problem itself

MIPS: finding the closest vector, fast

All of the above is scoring. But when you have ten million vectors and one query vector, how do you actually find the closest ones? This is the Maximum Inner Product Search problem, MIPS for short. Formally:

\[ \arg\max_{x \in X} \; q \cdot x \]

Doing this exactly costs \(O(N \cdot d)\) — compare the query to every vector, one component at a time. At ten million 768-dimensional vectors, that is billions of multiplications per query. Too slow.

So we use approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) indexes. These give up perfect accuracy in exchange for enormous speed. The most common are:

  • HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World): builds a multi-layer graph. Excellent recall, moderate memory.
  • IVF (Inverted File): clusters the vectors first, then only searches inside a few relevant clusters at query time. Cheap, less accurate.
  • PQ (Product Quantization): compresses vectors into short codes, sacrificing precision to fit huge indexes in RAM.
A subtle point

HNSW was originally proved on metric spaces (spaces where distance obeys nice rules like the triangle inequality). Inner product on non-normalised vectors is not a metric. If you run MIPS on non-normalised vectors with an HNSW index, correctness is not guaranteed — the graph might miss the true top-k. In practice most libraries handle this by normalising internally or using variants; but the theoretical guarantee is worth knowing.

Retrieval is scoring plus finding. Everything else — embeddings, hybrid systems, re-ranking — is just a way to make one or the other of those two things better.

Chapter Two

Embeddings, taken seriously.

An embedding is the whole substrate of dense retrieval. Choosing the right embedding model, training it on the right data, and refreshing it at the right cadence is often the single biggest lever in a RAG system after chunking.

§ 2.1  ·  the basic object

What an embedding actually is

An embedding is a list of numbers — typically several hundred of them — that represents a piece of text. The list is produced by a neural network. The number itself has no direct meaning; the meaning is in the relationships between vectors. Texts about similar things land near each other. Texts about different things land far apart.

Think of it as a huge multi-dimensional map. “Cat” is at one location. “Kitten” is nearby. “Dog” is a little further but still in the animals neighbourhood. “Photosynthesis” is on the other side of the map entirely.

The map is not designed. It emerges from training. Once trained, the network is a function: text goes in, a vector comes out, and semantically related texts get vectors that are close by whatever similarity metric you use.

§ 2.2  ·  why plain BERT is not enough

Sentence-BERT and the pooling problem

If you already know about BERT — the original transformer that revolutionised NLP — you might wonder: why not just take BERT and use its output for retrieval?

People tried. It did not work well. The reason is that BERT was trained to predict masked-out words in context. Its per-token vectors encode local meaning: the word in the context of the sentence around it. But those vectors were never optimised so that whole sentence representations of similar sentences would be close.

A common thing to try is mean-pooling: take BERT’s per-token vectors and average them together to get one sentence vector. This does something, but similarity scores from mean-pooled BERT vectors are terrible. Two sentences about completely different topics can produce mean vectors that look surprisingly alike, because averaging drowns out the discriminating information.

Sentence-BERT (SBERT) fixed this. The recipe: take a pre-trained BERT, add a pooling layer, then fine-tune it as a Siamese network on pairs of sentences labeled as similar or different. Training the model with a sentence-level objective forces the sentence vectors themselves to become meaningful. The lesson generalises: to get good sentence vectors, you must train with a sentence-level objective.

Modern embedders

Today’s state of the art (E5, BGE, GTE, Nomic Embed) uses the same principle but at much larger scale, with more sophisticated training data and often with instruction prefixes. You have to prepend something like "query: " or "passage: " to your text before embedding, because the model was trained expecting that prefix. Forgetting to do this silently degrades retrieval by 5–20% and is one of the most common footguns.

§ 2.3  ·  how embedders learn

Contrastive learning and InfoNCE

The training signal for a modern embedder is contrastive. You show the model triples like (query, correct answer, wrong answer) and ask it to pull the query and correct answer close while pushing the wrong answer away. Repeat billions of times across the internet and useful geometry emerges.

The dominant loss function is InfoNCE:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp\left(\text{sim}(q, d^+) / \tau\right)}{\sum_{i} \exp\left(\text{sim}(q, d_i) / \tau\right)} \]

Let us unpack it slowly.

\(q\) is the query vector. \(d^+\) is the positive — the correct match. The \(d_i\) in the sum include \(d^+\) itself along with several negatives (wrong matches). The function \(\text{sim}\) is similarity, usually cosine or dot product.

Look at the shape: numerator is the score of the correct pair; denominator is the sum of scores of all pairs, correct plus wrong. That is a softmax over the batch. Minimising \(\mathcal{L}\) means pushing the correct pair’s score up and the wrong pairs’ scores down.

The temperature knob

The \(\tau\) is temperature. It divides the similarities before the softmax. When \(\tau\) is small (say 0.05), similarities get amplified — the softmax becomes sharp and the loss is very sensitive to the ordering. When \(\tau\) is larger, the softmax is soft and the model treats near-ties as basically ties. Small temperature trains a picky model. Large temperature trains a forgiving one.

Where do negatives come from?

Two sources. In-batch negatives are essentially free: within a training batch of, say, 64 (query, positive) pairs, treat every other document in the batch as a negative for each query. This gets you 63 negatives at zero extra cost.

Hard negatives are documents that are almost right but are actually wrong. They are far more informative than random ones (a random document is obviously wrong; the model learns nothing). Mining hard negatives is expensive — you usually run a first-pass retriever like BM25, take documents that scored highly but were not actually relevant, and sometimes filter further with a cross-encoder. It is one of the most impactful things you can do to improve an embedder.

Interview-grade insight

Ten hard negatives usually help more than a hundred random ones. And the specific pipeline for mining them — not the choice of loss function — is often what separates a mediocre embedder from a great one.

§ 2.4  ·  the predecessor

Triplet loss, and why it faded

Before InfoNCE became standard, embedders were often trained with triplet loss:

\[ L = \max\left(0, \; \text{sim}(a, n) - \text{sim}(a, p) + \text{margin}\right) \]

Here \(a\) is the anchor (query), \(p\) is a positive, and \(n\) is a negative. The loss says: the anchor should be closer to the positive than to the negative by at least the margin. If that gap is already satisfied, the loss is zero.

The reason triplet loss faded: it only compares to one negative per step. InfoNCE compares to many negatives at once, which uses each training batch far more efficiently. Same compute, more signal.

§ 2.5  ·  the size question

Embedding dimensions and Matryoshka

Bigger embeddings feel like they should be better — more numbers, more capacity to encode meaning. But the honest picture is a trade-off table:

Once you cross about 1024 dimensions, quality gains slow down, but storage and latency keep growing linearly. For most production systems, 768 or 1024 is the sweet spot.

Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL)

A recent trick worth knowing: MRL trains a single embedding model such that the first \(k\) dimensions of the output are themselves a usable embedding, for many values of \(k\). You store the full 3072-dimensional vector once, and at query time you can truncate to 256 or 512 dimensions when you need speed, at the cost of only modest quality loss.

The name is Russian nesting dolls: each prefix of the vector is itself a valid, smaller vector. OpenAI’s text-embedding-3, Nomic, and Snowflake Arctic all use MRL. This turns dimension from a hard commit-once decision into a runtime knob.

§ 2.6  ·  a small detail with large consequences

Normalization: always be explicit

Normalising a vector means scaling it to length 1. It sounds like a detail. It is not.

If you are using cosine similarity, or if your vector index expects normalised inputs (many do, internally), you need normalised vectors to get correct rankings. If you feed un-normalised vectors into a dot-product-based index, longer vectors will score higher regardless of direction. This is catastrophic if all you cared about was direction.

The rule is simple: decide once, explicitly. Are your vectors normalised? Then use cosine or dot product interchangeably; ignore magnitudes. Are they not? Then dot product means something about magnitude, and you should know what. Never let this happen by accident.

§ 2.7  ·  the silent killer

Embedding drift, in two flavours

Model drift

You upgrade the embedding model. The old vectors and the new vectors live in different spaces. They are not compatible. If you index some documents with the old model and some with the new one, searches will return incoherent results — the geometry does not agree.

The fix is not clever. You must re-embed the entire corpus with the new model before switching. This is expensive at scale (imagine 500 million documents at a few milliseconds each on a GPU) and you need a rollout plan: dual-write to old and new indexes, verify quality on the new one, then cut over.

Distribution drift

Even without changing the model, the world changes. New products get released, new terminology emerges, the query distribution shifts. The embedder was trained on data from some point in time, and its accuracy on today’s data can slowly degrade. This is silent — there is no error message. Retrieval quality just goes down.

Track it. Keep an evaluation set. Re-run it monthly. Watch the numbers.

Operational canary

Also track the distribution of embedding norms over time. If it suddenly shifts, a preprocessing step upstream probably broke — maybe a text cleaner is now stripping something it should not. Embedding norms are a surprisingly good early warning.

§ 2.8  ·  making a general model your own

Domain adaptation

Off-the-shelf embedders are trained on general internet text. Your domain — legal, medical, financial, industrial — likely has vocabulary and phrasing the model has not seen well. Two ways to close that gap.

Continued pretraining

Keep training the base model on your corpus, without any labels. Methods like TSDAE (denoising autoencoder over sentence pairs) or GPL (Generative Pseudo-Labeling) let you continue teaching the model in a self-supervised way. You do not need query/document pairs.

Contrastive fine-tuning

If you have — or can generate — pairs of (query, relevant document), fine-tune with InfoNCE on those pairs. This is more direct and usually gives better results. Sources of pairs: user click logs, feedback signals, synthetic pairs from an LLM (“write a question this passage answers”).

A well fine-tuned 384-dimensional embedder on your data will often beat an off-the-shelf 3072-dimensional one. Data quality dominates dimension.

§ 2.9  ·  when text isn’t English

Multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval

Models like multilingual-E5, BGE-M3, and mE5 embed text from 100+ languages into a shared vector space. This unlocks two things: (a) multilingual retrieval, where users query in whatever language they know and get matches in that same language; and (b) cross-lingual retrieval, where an English query can retrieve French documents because both encode into the same semantic space.

The catch: quality is not uniform. A multilingual model that scores well on English and Chinese may be mediocre on Tamil or Swahili. The training data was not balanced across languages, and the model inherits that imbalance. Always evaluate on the specific languages you care about.

Transliteration is another wrinkle. Indian languages, for example, appear in both native scripts (Devanagari, Tamil script) and Latin-alphabet transliteration (“namaste”, “vanakkam”). Some multilingual models handle this gracefully; others do not. Test it.

§ 2.10  ·  a different geometry

Late interaction: ColBERT

Everything so far assumed one vector per document. ColBERT throws that out. It stores one vector per token. A document with 200 tokens becomes 200 vectors.

At query time, you compute similarity through a scoring function called MaxSim: for each query token, find the maximum similarity to any document token, then sum those maxima across query tokens.

\[ \text{score}(q, d) = \sum_{i \in q} \max_{j \in d} \; \text{sim}(q_i, d_j) \]

Why bother with all these vectors? Because it captures fine-grained matches. If your query has ten tokens and only three of them matter, MaxSim will still find the document where those three tokens have strong matches somewhere — even if the document is long and the matching tokens are scattered.

The obvious cost is storage. ColBERT indexes are roughly 100 times larger than dual-encoder indexes. Later versions — ColBERT-v2 and PLAID — compress the token vectors by clustering them and storing residuals against centroids, which brings the storage cost way down without sacrificing much quality.

Where ColBERT fits

ColBERT sits between bi-encoders (fast, coarser) and cross-encoders (accurate, expensive). Retrieval quality is often close to cross-encoders, at latency close to bi-encoders. It is a strong choice when you need better quality than dual-encoders but cannot afford cross-encoder re-ranking on every candidate.

Chapter Three

Document processing, or: garbage in, garbage out.

The most common cause of a bad answer in a RAG system is not the model, not the embedder, and not the vector database. It is that the document was mangled during ingestion. This chapter is about what actually goes wrong before retrieval even begins.

§ 3.1  ·  the honest starting point

Retrieval failures are usually ingestion failures

When someone reports that a RAG system gave a wrong answer, the instinct is to blame the retriever or the LLM. In practice, most such failures trace back much earlier. The passage the model was reading was already broken: a paragraph split across a page break got cut in half, a table lost its column structure, a footer bled into the body text, a scanned PDF was silently text-empty and never chunked at all.

A clean ingestion pipeline is not glamorous work, but it is where the largest quality wins live.

§ 3.2  ·  the format from hell

PDF parsing

PDF is a display format, not a document format. It describes where marks appear on a page — “the letter ‘A’ at coordinate (72, 480), in Helvetica, 12pt” — not what those marks mean semantically. Extracting structured text from a PDF is fundamentally a reverse-engineering task.

Two broad approaches:

Text-dump extractors

Libraries like pypdf pull the text stream as it exists in the PDF. Fast, cheap, and often wrong for anything with layout — two-column pages will interleave columns character by character; tables will come out as unreadable soup.

Structure-aware extractors

Libraries like unstructured.io, PyMuPDF, pdfplumber, and commercial tools like LlamaParse and Docling try to reconstruct the logical structure. They understand columns, headings, lists, tables, footnotes. They are slower and more expensive, but the output is actually useful.

The right choice depends on the corpus. Simple linear reports? A text-dump extractor may be fine. Financial filings, academic papers, medical documents with tables? You need structure-aware.

§ 3.3  ·  when text is actually pixels

OCR: reading text that isn’t really text

A significant fraction of real-world PDFs are scans — images of pages, with no underlying text layer. You cannot extract from them because there is nothing to extract. You have to read the pixels. That is Optical Character Recognition, OCR.

The main choices:

  • Tesseract: open-source baseline. Free, reasonable for clean printed text in Latin scripts. Struggles with rotated pages, non-Latin scripts, and low-quality scans.
  • PaddleOCR: strong on Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and other non-Latin scripts. Open-source.
  • TrOCR: transformer-based OCR from Microsoft. More accurate on tricky text (handwriting, unusual fonts) at higher compute cost.
  • Commercial services like Amazon Textract, Azure Document Intelligence, and Google Document AI: expensive per page, but the best available accuracy, especially for forms and hybrid documents where text and structure both matter.
Practical routing

A sensible policy: try text-layer extraction first. If the result is empty or suspiciously short, run OCR. For most documents this saves the OCR cost entirely; for scanned ones it triggers automatically.

§ 3.4  ·  the other messy format

HTML parsing

Web pages are half content, half navigation and advertising. Naive HTML-to-text extraction picks up the whole soup: menus, footers, cookie banners, sidebars, and the article itself all jumbled together. The article is 10% of the words on the page; the other 90% is noise.

Tools like trafilatura and readability-lxml attempt to identify the main content region and return only that. They use heuristics about text density, HTML structure, and link ratios — the article body has lots of text and few links; the nav has few words but many links.

For JavaScript-heavy sites where content is rendered on the client, you need a headless browser like Playwright to actually run the page before extracting.

Whatever you use, preserve semantic structure: headings, lists, tables. You will want these downstream for smart chunking.

§ 3.5  ·  the hardest case

Table extraction

Tables are where document parsers go to die. A table encodes information along two axes — rows and columns — and a naive text extraction flattens it into one dimension. All the meaning is lost.

Approaches, roughly in order of complexity:

  • Camelot and Tabula: work well when the table has clear ruled lines. Fail on borderless tables.
  • TableTransformer and DETR-based models: use vision to detect table structure in images. Work on unruled and image-based tables. Not perfect.
  • LLM-based extraction: hand a table image or region to a vision-capable LLM and ask for the structured output. Slow and expensive, but handles arbitrary layouts including nested cells.

Once extracted, represent tables as Markdown or CSV, not plain text. Markdown preserves the row/column structure in a way both humans and language models can read.

The rite of passage

Sooner or later you will meet a merged cell inside a table inside an image inside a rotated PDF page. It is not a bug in your pipeline; it is a normal document. Have a strategy: either fail gracefully with a clear error, route to a more capable extractor, or flag for human review.

§ 3.6  ·  the boring but critical work

Cleaning and normalisation

Raw extracted text is almost always dirty. Real cleaning steps include:

  • Stripping repeating boilerplate: headers, footers, page numbers, watermarks. If “Confidential — Q4 2024” appears on every page, it should not be repeated in every chunk.
  • Normalising whitespace: replace runs of spaces and newlines with single ones, remove trailing whitespace.
  • Unicode normalisation to NFC form — the same character can be encoded multiple ways in Unicode, and you want them collapsed to a canonical form so string matching works.
  • Normalising typography: curly quotes to straight, em-dashes to hyphens if you need consistency, ligatures unpacked.

None of this is exciting. All of it changes retrieval quality noticeably.

§ 3.7  ·  removing the copies

Deduplication, in three tiers

Real corpora are full of near-copies. The same document is uploaded twice. A press release appears on five sites. A template email is repeated across a thousand messages. If you leave them in, the retriever returns the same information ten times and the LLM writes an answer based on ten repetitions of one thing.

Exact deduplication

Hash each document (SHA-256 is fine), and drop repeats. Fast, cheap, catches exact duplicates only. One trailing whitespace character defeats it.

Near-duplicate detection

For documents that are almost the same — different footers, whitespace, minor edits — use MinHash combined with Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH). MinHash gives you a signature of each document’s content, and LSH lets you find similar signatures efficiently across billions of documents. It is the industry-standard method for this.

Semantic deduplication

Embed each document, and drop pairs with very high similarity (say, cosine > 0.95). Catches paraphrases and translations of the same content. Expensive at scale.

At corpus scale, near-duplicate removal improves retrieval quality more than any embedding-model upgrade. It is the highest-value change most teams have never made.

§ 3.8  ·  the invisible half

Metadata is retrieval infrastructure

A chunk of text without metadata is a floating fact. A chunk with metadata — author, date, document type, source URL, access permissions, tags — can be filtered, audited, and trusted.

What metadata to always capture:

  • Provenance: source URL or file path, ingestion timestamp, parser version.
  • Content: title, author, publication date, document type.
  • Access: who is allowed to see this? Group memberships, ACLs, sensitivity level.
  • Structure: which section of which document this chunk came from.

Filterable metadata makes queries like “find me policy documents from 2024 that I am allowed to read” possible. Without it, you retrieve blindly and hope.

§ 3.9  ·  preserving the shape

Document hierarchies

Documents are not flat. A book has chapters, sections, subsections, paragraphs, sentences. When you chunk a document you break it up, but you can preserve the hierarchy: store the tree document → section → paragraph → chunk, and give every chunk a reference back up the tree.

Why bother? It unlocks parent-child retrieval: retrieve at the fine-grained chunk level for precision, then expand the answer using the parent section for context. The model sees not just the matching sentence but the paragraph it lived in.

Preserving section headings is the minimum. If you throw away structural information at parse time, you cannot get it back.

§ 3.10  ·  what actually happens

Production is stranger than you think

Every idealised pipeline meets reality and cracks. Some of the things you will actually encounter:

  • About 10% of PDFs will be scans with no text layer. About 5% will have rotated pages, mixed languages, or embedded fonts that break extraction.
  • Enterprises will send you .msg (Outlook emails), .eml, .pptx, old .doc files (not .docx), inside password-protected zips, inside SharePoint libraries with weird permission models.
  • Tables inside merged cells inside images inside PDFs. It is not exotic; it is a Tuesday.

Three practices that pay off, always:

  1. Separate storage layers with lineage: raw bytes, parsed text, chunks, embeddings, each with IDs that link back up the chain. When your chunker changes, you re-chunk from parsed text — you do not re-parse from raw.
  2. Version every step: parser version, chunker version, embedder version. Attach them to each chunk as metadata. When you see a bad answer, you can trace exactly which parser produced it.
  3. Log parse-failure rates by source: it is the canary. When a source suddenly starts failing, something changed upstream — a new file format, a corrupted upload pipeline, a scanned batch. You catch it early.
The debugging playbook

When a user reports a wrong answer, walk the pipeline backwards: check the generation, then the retrieved chunks, then the chunker, then the parser, then the raw file. Nine times out of ten the problem is upstream of the retriever. The tenth time is the interesting one.
