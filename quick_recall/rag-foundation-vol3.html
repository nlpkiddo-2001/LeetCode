The order you return them, the answer you write, the proof that it worked.

Volume III. Continues from the second notebook. The chapters on chunking, vector databases, and query processing built the machinery that finds candidates. This volume is about what happens after they are found.

By now we can score documents, embed them, cut them into chunks, store billions of vectors, and reshape a user’s question. What we have not done is turn any of it into an answer — or checked whether the answer is any good. Three concepts finish the pipeline: reranking (put the best candidate on top), generation (write the grounded answer), and evaluation (prove the whole thing works). The last one is the chapter everyone skips, and the one that separates a demo from a system.

A production RAG pipeline is a funnel. Each stage is cheaper per item but sees more items than the next; each hands a smaller, better set to the one after it. This volume covers the bottom half.

Chapter Seven

Reranking: the second look.

The first-stage retriever is built for speed and recall — it casts a wide net and lets some junk through on purpose. The reranker is the second pass: slower, sharper, and applied only to the shortlist. Getting the ordering right at the top is often the single highest-leverage change in the whole pipeline.

§ 7.0  ·  why two stages exist at all

Recall first, precision second

There is a tension no single retriever resolves well. To search a billion vectors in milliseconds you need an index that is fast — and fast means approximate, which means the scoring is coarse. A coarse scorer is good at not missing the right document (recall) but bad at putting it in exactly the right spot (precision at the very top).

So production systems split the job. The first stage — ANN over embeddings, or BM25, or both — is tuned for recall: get the relevant documents somewhere in the top few hundred. The second stage — the reranker — is tuned for precision: take that shortlist and reorder it so the best few rise to the top.

The economics are what make this work. The first stage is cheap per item, so it can afford to look at everything. The reranker is expensive per item, so it can only afford to look at the shortlist — but the shortlist is small (100, not a billion), so the total cost stays bounded. You pay for accuracy exactly where it matters and nowhere else.

The number that moves

A first-stage retriever might put the right answer at rank 30. That is a recall success — it is in the top 100 — but a precision failure, because you only send the top 5 to the model. The reranker’s entire job is to move that rank-30 document to rank 1. When people say “adding a reranker fixed our RAG,” this is almost always the mechanism.

§ 7.1  ·  the architectural fork

Bi-encoders and cross-encoders

To understand reranking you have to understand why the first stage cannot just be accurate to begin with. It comes down to when the query and the document meet.

A bi-encoder embeds the query and the document separately, into the same space, and compares them with a cheap operation (dot product or cosine). Because the document embeddings do not depend on the query, you can compute them once at ingestion time and store them. At query time you only embed the query and do a fast similarity search. This is the whole reason vector databases are possible — and the reason they are approximate. The query and document never actually interact; they only meet as two finished vectors.

A cross-encoder does the opposite. It concatenates the query and the document into a single input — [query] [SEP] [document] — and runs them through the model together, so every query token can attend to every document token. The output is a single relevance score. This is dramatically more accurate, because the model can reason about how the specific query relates to the specific document. But nothing can be precomputed: the score depends on the pair, so you need one full forward pass per document.

Bi-encoder — retrieve

Two separate passes, no interaction. Precompute every document. Fast enough for a billion.

Cross-encoder — rerank

One joint pass, full interaction. Nothing precomputed — so only ever run on a shortlist.

That is the entire trade in one picture. The bi-encoder is the retriever from Volume II. The cross-encoder is the reranker. You use the first to get from a billion to a hundred, and the second to get from a hundred to five. Neither replaces the other; the pipeline needs both.

§ 7.2  ·  the workhorse reranker

Cross-encoder reranking in practice

A cross-encoder reranker takes the top-\(K\) candidates from the first stage, forms \(K\) query-document pairs, scores each one, and sorts by score. The families you will meet by name are the MS MARCO-trained MiniLM cross-encoders (small, fast, a strong default), the multilingual bge-reranker models, and hosted APIs like Cohere Rerank and Jina Reranker. They all share the same shape: pair in, relevance score out.

The one number you have to pick is \(K\), the shortlist size. It is a direct latency-versus-quality dial:

\[ \text{rerank latency} \approx K \times (\text{cost of one cross-encoder pass}) \]

Rerank 100 candidates and you get almost all the quality a reranker can offer, at 100 forward passes of latency. Rerank 1000 and you buy a little more recall for ten times the cost. Rerank 10 and you have barely given the reranker anything to reorder. In most systems \(K\) between 50 and 200 is the sweet spot; batch the pairs so the passes run in parallel on the accelerator rather than one at a time.

The reranker cannot fix what retrieval missed

A reranker only reorders the shortlist it is handed. If the right document sits at rank 4000 because the first stage never surfaced it, no reranker will find it — it is not in the candidate set. This is why the first stage is tuned for recall, not precision: its only job is to make sure the answer is somewhere in the top-\(K\). Reranking is worthless if retrieval recall is bad. Fix recall first.

§ 7.3  ·  the middle ground

Late interaction (ColBERT-style)

Bi-encoders are fast but blunt; cross-encoders are sharp but slow. Late interaction sits between them, and if you read the embeddings chapter in Volume I, ColBERT already came up — here is where it earns its place in the pipeline.

Instead of collapsing a document into a single vector, a late-interaction model keeps one vector per token. At query time it also keeps one vector per query token. The relevance score is computed with MaxSim: for each query token, find its best-matching document token, then sum those best matches.

\[ \text{score}(q, d) = \sum_{i \in q} \max_{j \in d} \; \big( E_{q_i} \cdot E_{d_j} \big) \]
Each query token picks its most similar document token; the matches are summed

This captures token-level interaction — the word “jaguar” in the query can lock onto “jaguar” in the document even if the rest of the sentence differs — without a full joint forward pass. Because document token vectors can be precomputed and stored, ColBERT can even serve as a first-stage retriever, not just a reranker. The cost is storage: one vector per token is far more than one vector per chunk, which is why late interaction lives or dies on aggressive vector compression.

§ 7.4  ·  asking the model to judge

LLM-based reranking

The most flexible reranker is a general-purpose LLM prompted to judge relevance. There are three shapes, trading cost against quality.

Pointwise

Show the model one query and one document, ask for a relevance score (say 0–10 or a yes/no). Simple and parallelisable, but the model scores each document in isolation, with no sense of the others — so the scores are noisy and hard to compare across documents.

Pairwise

Show the model two documents and ask which is more relevant. This is what humans are actually good at, and the judgments are reliable — but ranking \(K\) documents by comparisons is expensive, closer to a sort than a single pass.

Listwise

Show the model the query and a numbered list of documents at once, and ask it to return the list reordered by relevance. This is the idea behind RankGPT. It is efficient and the model sees all candidates together, so it can make relative judgments — but long lists overflow the context window, so listwise reranking usually runs in overlapping windows that are then stitched together.

When it is worth it

LLM rerankers are slower and pricier than a dedicated cross-encoder, and for pure relevance a good cross-encoder often matches them. Reach for an LLM reranker when relevance depends on reasoning the cross-encoder cannot do — multi-hop logic, instructions in the query (“prefer official sources,” “most recent first”), or nuanced domain judgment. Otherwise the cross-encoder is the better default.

§ 7.5  ·  merging ranked lists

Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Hybrid search — dense retrieval plus BM25 — gives you two ranked lists of the same documents, with scores on completely different scales. A cosine similarity of 0.82 and a BM25 score of 14.3 cannot be added directly; one would swamp the other. You need a way to combine rankings without trusting the raw scores.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion does exactly this, and it is beautifully simple. For each document, ignore the scores entirely and use only its rank in each list:

\[ \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{i \in \text{lists}} \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_i(d)} \]
k is a small constant, conventionally 60; rank starts at 1

A document ranked first in a list contributes \(1/(k+1)\); ranked tenth, \(1/(k+10)\). Sum a document’s contributions across all the lists it appears in, and sort by that sum. Documents that rank highly in either retriever float up; documents that rank highly in both float up hardest. The constant \(k\) softens the influence of the very top ranks so a single list cannot dominate.

RRF needs no score normalisation, no tuning, and no training. It works for two lists or ten, dense and sparse, or several rerankers combined. It is the default fusion method in most hybrid systems for a reason: it is almost impossible to make it perform badly.

§ 7.6  ·  relevance is not enough

Maximal Marginal Relevance

Rank purely by relevance and you hit a subtle failure: the top five results are often five near-duplicates of the same passage. Each is individually relevant, but together they tell the model one thing five times, wasting the context budget and starving it of the other facts the answer needs.

Maximal Marginal Relevance fixes this by rewarding a candidate for being relevant to the query and different from what has already been selected. You build the result set greedily, at each step picking the document that maximises:

\[ \text{MMR} = \arg\max_{d \in R \setminus S} \Big[\, \lambda \cdot \text{sim}(d, q) \;-\; (1-\lambda)\,\max_{s \in S}\, \text{sim}(d, s) \,\Big] \]
R is the candidate pool, S the already-chosen set, λ balances relevance against diversity

The first term is relevance to the query; the second penalises similarity to documents already picked. With \(\lambda = 1\) you get pure relevance (and the duplicates come back); with \(\lambda = 0\) you get pure diversity (and lose the plot). Values around 0.5–0.7 usually give a result set that is both on-topic and non-redundant. MMR matters most for broad or exploratory queries where the answer draws on several distinct facts.

§ 7.7  ·  turning scores into decisions

Calibration and the “nothing relevant” case

Reranker scores are not probabilities. A cross-encoder might output 8.2 for a great match and −3.1 for a poor one, but those numbers have no absolute meaning — they are only useful for ordering. This becomes a problem the moment you want to make a decision, not just a ranking.

The most important decision is the relevance floor: what if none of the candidates are actually any good? Every retriever returns a top-\(k\) even for a query the corpus cannot answer. If you pass those irrelevant chunks to the model anyway, you have handed it the raw materials for a confident, wrong, well-cited answer. Far better to detect “nothing here is relevant” and let the model say so.

To do that you need calibrated scores. The usual route is to fit a logistic (sigmoid) mapping from raw reranker scores to a 0–1 relevance probability on a labelled set, then set a threshold: below it, treat the result as no-answer. This is unglamorous and easy to skip, and skipping it is a leading cause of hallucinated answers to unanswerable questions.

A reranker changes the order; calibration changes what you do with the order. The first improves good answers. The second prevents bad ones.

Chapter Eight

Generation: where it all gets spent.

Every stage so far exists to produce one thing: a small set of relevant passages. Generation is where that set becomes an answer — or fails to. Perfect retrieval feeding a careless prompt still produces a bad system. This is the least glamorous chapter and the one where the most quality is quietly lost.

§ 8.0  ·  the last mile

The generator can only be as good as its context

It is tempting to think the model does the hard part — you retrieve some text, hand it over, and the LLM figures it out. In reality the generation step is where a surprising amount of engineering lives. The model does not see your corpus, your reranker scores, or your intentions. It sees exactly one thing: the prompt you assembled. Everything about that prompt — what you put in it, in what order, with what instructions — determines the answer.

Two systems with identical retrieval can produce completely different answers because one assembled the context thoughtfully and the other dumped ten chunks into a template. This chapter is about that assembly, and about the failure modes — hallucination, distraction, refusal — that live here.

§ 8.1  ·  building the prompt

Context assembly

Assembly is the step that turns a ranked list of chunks into the text block the model reads. The moving parts are: how many chunks to include, in what order, how to separate them, and what instructions to wrap around them.

How many. More context is not better. Beyond a handful of chunks you start adding marginally-relevant material that dilutes the signal and invites the model to wander. Most systems land between three and eight chunks; the right number is an empirical choice, not a default.

Separation and attribution. Give each chunk a clear delimiter and a stable source label — a document title, an ID, a URL. This does double duty: it helps the model keep sources distinct, and it is what makes grounded citation possible later. A common shape is to number the sources and prefix each chunk with its number so the model can cite [3] directly.

Instructions. The wrapper text matters as much as the chunks. It tells the model to answer from the provided context, to cite its sources, and — critically — what to do when the context does not contain the answer. We return to that in § 8.5.

§ 8.2  ·  position is not neutral

Lost in the middle

Here is a result that surprises people the first time they meet it. When you place relevant information inside a long context, the model uses it far better if it sits near the beginning or the end than if it sits in the middle. Accuracy as a function of the answer’s position traces a U: strong at the edges, sagging in the centre.

The practical consequence is a specific ordering rule. Do not put your best chunk in the middle of a long context. Put the most relevant material first and last, and let the weaker candidates fill the centre — the position where they will do the least harm. Some systems go further and reorder reranked chunks into an explicit “strongest at the ends” arrangement before assembly.

This is also an argument against overstuffing. A shorter context has no bad middle to get lost in. If you only include the four chunks that matter, position stops being a problem at all.

§ 8.3  ·  the token account

Context-window budgeting

The context window is a fixed account, and everything competes for it: the system instructions, the conversation history, the retrieved chunks, and the space the model needs to actually write the answer. Spend it all on retrieved context and the model has no room to respond; spend too little and you starve it of evidence.

A useful way to think about it as a simple subtraction:

\[ \text{budget}_{\text{context}} = W - \text{tokens}_{\text{system}} - \text{tokens}_{\text{history}} - \text{tokens}_{\text{answer}} \]
W is the model’s window; reserve the answer allowance before you fill the rest

Reserve the answer allowance first — decide how long an answer you need and protect that space — then fill the remainder with reranked chunks in priority order until the budget runs out. When a chunk does not fit, drop it rather than truncating it mid-sentence; a half-chunk is often worse than no chunk. Large windows do not make budgeting obsolete, because “lost in the middle” means a filled 200k window frequently answers worse than a curated 8k one.

§ 8.4  ·  more signal per token

Context compression

Retrieved chunks are rarely all signal. A chunk that matched on one sentence might carry four more that are irrelevant to this query. Compression is the step that strips a chunk down to the part that actually earns its place in the budget.

Extractive compression

Score the individual sentences within each retrieved chunk against the query — the same cross-encoder from Chapter 7 works well — and keep only the sentences that clear a threshold. This is cheap and safe: nothing is rewritten, so nothing is fabricated. It simply throws away the padding.

Abstractive compression

Ask a small model to rewrite the retrieved context into a shorter, query-focused summary before it reaches the generator. Methods in the LLMLingua family push this further, dropping low-information tokens to compress prompts several-fold. More powerful than extraction, but it introduces a second generation step that can itself distort or drop facts — so it needs its own faithfulness checks.

Compression is a budget decision, not a quality one

The reason to compress is that context is scarce and its middle is weak. If your chunks are already tight and you are nowhere near the window limit, compression adds cost and risk for little gain. Reach for it when you are budget-constrained or when retrieved chunks are long and noisy — not by default.

§ 8.5  ·  correct is not the same as grounded

Grounding, citation, and faithfulness

There is a distinction at the heart of RAG that is easy to blur. An answer can be correct — true in the world — while being ungrounded — not actually supported by the retrieved context. The model knew it from pre-training and said it anyway. For a trivia bot that is fine. For a system that must be auditable — legal, medical, financial — it is a failure, because you cannot show where the answer came from, and next time the same behaviour will produce something confidently wrong.

Faithfulness (or groundedness) is the property that every claim in the answer is entailed by the provided context. It is the thing RAG is actually for. You encourage it three ways:

  • Instruction. Tell the model explicitly to answer only from the context, and to say it does not know when the context is silent. Then give it permission to refuse — models over-answer partly because prompts never allow the alternative.
  • Citation. Require the model to attribute each claim to a source label from the assembled context ([2], [5]). Citations make grounding checkable by a human and by an evaluator, and the act of citing nudges the model to stay close to the evidence.
  • Verification. For high-stakes systems, run a second pass that checks each sentence of the answer against the cited chunk and flags anything unsupported.

RAG reduces hallucination; it does not abolish it. The model can still misread context, blend two sources, or fall back on its parameters when the context is thin. Grounding is what turns “probably right” into “right, and here is why.”

§ 8.6  ·  when one pass is not enough

Iterative and agentic generation

Everything so far assumes a single shot: retrieve once, generate once. For hard questions that is not enough — the answer needs facts that no single query surfaces, or the first retrieval comes back weak and the system should notice and try again. A family of patterns closes the loop.

Query decomposition. Break a compound question (“how did the two companies’ margins compare after the merger?”) into sub-questions, retrieve for each, and synthesise. Each sub-question is easier to retrieve for than the whole.

Corrective RAG (CRAG). After retrieving, grade the results. If they look strong, generate. If they look weak, take a corrective action — rewrite the query, widen the search, or fall back to web search — before generating. The system refuses to answer from bad evidence.

Self-RAG. Interleave retrieval and generation, letting the model decide when it needs to retrieve and critique its own output against what it found, retrieving again if a claim is unsupported. Generation becomes a loop of draft, check, and refine rather than a single forward pass.

Single-pass RAG answers the question you asked with the documents you found. Agentic RAG decides whether those were the right documents, and the right question, before it commits to an answer.

Chapter Nine

Evaluation: the chapter everyone skips.

A RAG system has many knobs and no obvious way to tell whether turning one helped. Without measurement, every change is a guess and every “improvement” is a vibe. This chapter is how you replace the vibe with a number — and how you make sure the number measures the right thing.

§ 9.0  ·  two failures, measured apart

Retrieval and generation fail differently

When a RAG system gives a bad answer, there are two very different causes, and they need very different fixes. Either retrieval failed — the right context was never found, so the model never had a chance — or generation failed — the right context was right there and the model still produced a bad answer.

The single most useful discipline in RAG evaluation is measuring these two halves separately. A single end-to-end score tells you the system is bad; it does not tell you which half to fix, and so it does not tell you what to do on Monday. Split the metrics, and every failure points at its own cause.

§ 9.1  ·  scoring the shortlist

Retrieval metrics

Retrieval metrics need a labelled set: queries paired with the documents that are known to be relevant. Given that, four metrics do most of the work.

Recall@k and Precision@k

Recall@k — already met in Volume II — is the fraction of all relevant documents that appear in your top-\(k\). It answers “did we find the good stuff?” Precision@k is the fraction of your top-\(k\) that is relevant — “how much of what we returned was junk?” For the first stage you care most about recall; for the final set fed to the model you care about precision.

MRR — Mean Reciprocal Rank

When there is one right answer and you care where it landed, MRR is the metric. For each query take the reciprocal of the rank of the first relevant result, and average across queries:

\[ \text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{i=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{\text{rank}_i} \]

First place scores 1, second place \(1/2\), fifth place \(1/5\). MRR rewards getting the right answer near the very top and is unforgiving about burying it — exactly the property a reranker is meant to improve.

nDCG — Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain

MRR only sees the first relevant hit and treats relevance as binary. nDCG handles the general case: many relevant documents, each with a graded relevance, and position mattering throughout the list. It sums the relevance of each result, discounted by how far down it sits, then normalises against the best possible ordering:

\[ \text{DCG@k} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)} \qquad \text{nDCG@k} = \frac{\text{DCG@k}}{\text{IDCG@k}} \]
The log discount makes a relevant result at rank 2 worth more than one at rank 9; IDCG is the ideal ordering

nDCG runs from 0 to 1, where 1 is a perfect ranking. It is the standard metric in information retrieval precisely because it captures everything the simpler metrics leave out — graded relevance and full-list position — in a single comparable number.

§ 9.2  ·  the data you evaluate against

Building the evaluation set

Every metric above needs ground truth, and ground truth is the part nobody wants to build. You need a set of realistic queries, each paired with the documents that answer them and, ideally, a reference answer. There is a chicken-and-egg problem: you want to evaluate the system before you have users, but real queries come from users.

The common bootstrap is synthetic generation: walk your corpus, and for each chunk ask an LLM to write a question that this chunk answers. Now you have query-document pairs for free, and the document that generated the question is by construction its relevant result. It is not as good as real traffic — synthetic questions are cleaner and more literal than what humans type — but it is enough to start measuring, and it is infinitely better than nothing.

Once real queries arrive, mine them. Sample actual user questions, label the good and bad answers, and fold them into the eval set. A living eval set that tracks real traffic is one of the most valuable assets a RAG system has — it is what makes every future change measurable.

§ 9.3  ·  scoring the answer

The RAG triad

Retrieval metrics score the context. Generation metrics score the answer — but “is this answer good” is too vague to measure directly. The widely-used decomposition (popularised by frameworks like RAGAS and TruLens) breaks it into three relationships between the query, the retrieved context, and the answer.

Context relevance — is the retrieved context actually on-topic for the query? A retrieval-side check. Faithfulness — is every claim in the answer supported by the context? The grounding property from Chapter 8. Answer relevance — does the answer actually address what was asked, rather than being true-but-beside-the-point?

The three localise faults precisely. Low context relevance is a retrieval problem. High context relevance but low faithfulness is a generation problem — the model had the goods and ignored them. Everything grounded but low answer relevance means the model answered a different question. One triad, three diagnoses.

§ 9.4  ·  grading at scale

LLM-as-judge

Computing the triad by hand does not scale — you would need a human to read every answer against every context. So the standard practice is to use a strong LLM as the grader: give it the query, the context, and the answer, and ask it to score faithfulness or relevance, usually with a rubric and a required justification.

It works well and it is cheap, but the judge has biases you must design around:

  • Position bias. In pairwise comparisons, judges favour whichever answer came first. Mitigate by running both orderings and averaging.
  • Verbosity bias. Judges tend to rate longer answers higher regardless of quality. Watch for it, and instruct against it.
  • Self-preference. A judge tends to prefer text generated by itself or its own model family. Where it matters, use a different model to judge than to generate.

The essential guardrail: validate the judge against humans. Have people grade a sample, check that the LLM judge agrees with them, and only then trust it at scale. An unvalidated judge is just a second opinion of unknown quality — measure its agreement before you build on it.

§ 9.5  ·  the eval that never stops

Online evaluation and drift

Everything so far is offline evaluation — a fixed eval set, run before you ship. It tells you whether a change is better than what you had. It cannot tell you how the system behaves on live traffic, which is always messier than any eval set.

Online evaluation watches the running system. Implicit signals — did the user rephrase and try again, did they click the citation, did they copy the answer, thumbs up or down — are noisy individually but powerful in aggregate. A/B tests compare two configurations on real users and settle arguments that offline metrics leave open.

And a RAG system decays even if you touch nothing. The corpus grows and shifts, user questions move to new topics, the world changes underneath fixed documents. This is drift, and the only defence is continuous measurement: keep the eval set current, watch the online signals for a slide, and re-check when the distribution moves.

Offline eval tells you if a change is good before you ship it. Online eval tells you if the system is still good after the world has moved. You need both, and you never stop running either.

§ 9.6  ·  the whole pipeline, in order

Nine chapters, one system

Step back and the three volumes trace a single path. You started by scoring documents — TF-IDF, BM25, dense, hybrid — and turning text into vectors with embeddings, then wrestled raw documents into clean text. That was Volume I: the raw materials.

Volume II made it retrievable: chunking cut documents into the units you index, vector databases stored the billions of resulting vectors and found nearest neighbours in milliseconds, and query processing reshaped the user’s question into something the retriever could match. That was the top of the funnel.

And this volume finished it. Reranking took the wide, high-recall candidate set and sharpened it to a precise few. Generation assembled those few into a prompt and produced a grounded, cited answer. Evaluation wrapped the whole thing in measurement, so you can tell — not guess — whether any of it works, and which stage to fix when it does not.

Each stage is a lever. The systems that win are rarely the ones with the fanciest single component; they are the ones that measured every stage, found the weakest one, and fixed it — then did it again. Retrieval, reranking, generation, evaluation. Build the funnel, then watch it.
